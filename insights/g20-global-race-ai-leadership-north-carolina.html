G20: The Global Race for AI Leadership - North Carolina

Published September 1, 2026

Two G20 tracks closed in North Carolina within hours of each other on Tuesday. One failed to agree on anything, because China would not sign. The other asked the world to agree not to write rules, and the United States says China signed that one.

In Plain English

The G20 is the group of the twenty largest economies. Its ministers meet through the year to agree on shared rules, and those meetings normally end with a joint statement.

In Asheville, the finance ministers could not produce one, because China would not sign. That sounds procedural. In practice it means the countries that set the terms for trade, currency policy and sovereign debt do not currently agree, and anyone operating across borders is planning around a disagreement rather than a settled answer.

In Chapel Hill, the United States asked the other members to promise not to write new AI laws unless something genuinely new comes along, and not to create new agencies to police it. Late in the day, the White House said China had agreed to it, though China itself has not confirmed that and nobody has published the document. So the same country that blocked one statement in the morning reportedly signed the other in the afternoon.

Either way the promise is voluntary. Nobody would be bound by it, and Europe is not repealing the AI laws it already has. The likely result is the same AI product facing strict rules in one country and almost none in another.

The part that reaches ordinary life fastest is electricity. AI runs in data centers, and data centers consume enormous amounts of power. Speaker after speaker said there will not be enough of it, and not enough electricians and construction workers to build what has already been promised. Where that power comes from, and who pays for it, is being decided town by town right now. If a data center plugs into the existing grid and competes with houses for the same supply, residential bills go up. If the operator builds or buys its own generation, they may not. That argument is already underway in North Carolina.

The rest of this is the detail, and what it means if you are planning technology investment.

What happened in North Carolina

The G20 Innovation Ministerial is running September 1 and 2 at the Carolina Inn in Chapel Hill, co-hosted by Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and White House Office of Science and Technology Policy Director Michael Kratsios. The United States holds the rotating G20 presidency this year, so Washington sets the agenda for every ministerial ahead of the leaders' summit in Miami in December.

The deliverable is a non-binding framework called the Carolina Principles. Signatories commit to invest in foundational research, expand commercial opportunities for emerging technology, and, in the operative clause, reserve new regulation for novel considerations when writing AI rules. Reuters reported that members are also being asked not to stand up new bodies to oversee AI development.

Then the day turned. Kratsios told reporters that the Chinese government had signed the principles, but did not provide a copy of the document, and the Chinese embassy did not immediately respond to a request for comment. China's Minister of Science and Technology, Yin Hejun, represented Beijing at the ministerial, and Kratsios described his bilateral meeting with his Chinese counterpart as a good one.

That claim deserves careful handling. It is a reported endorsement, not a confirmed one. The full text of the principles has not been published, no signatory list has been released, and Beijing has not independently said anything. Until it does, the accurate status is that the United States says China signed.

If it holds, it is the most consequential thing to come out of Tuesday. It would mean the two countries competing hardest over models, chips and compute broadly agree on how the world should govern the technology, or at least on how little it should. It also relocates the fault line. The interesting split stops being Washington against Beijing and becomes light-touch against codified, with Brussels on the other side of it. France, Germany and Italy sent ministers who are already bound by the EU AI Act, which created exactly the kind of regulator the framework asks governments to avoid. Canada said before the meeting that its delegation would advocate for balancing innovation with public trust and safety.

Day one belonged to the technology and digital ministers, with the industry appearing largely by video. Elon Musk, the first invited speaker, criticized EU technology regulation and said innovation requires an environment where new things are default legal rather than default illegal. He warned that consensus expects a significant power shortfall next year, not in the distant future, and said he believed AI would increase the size of the global economy by 20 to 30 percent, roughly 20 to 30 trillion dollars a year.

Mark Zuckerberg said the buildout would require hundreds of thousands and possibly millions of skilled trade jobs, then noted that Meta itself cannot find the tradespeople it needs to build the data centers it has already committed to, naming carpenters and electricians specifically. David Sacks argued that a thicket of laws already applies to AI and that regulating frontier models as closely as aircraft manufacturing or pharmaceuticals would be a mistake. Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis told the ministers that human-level AI would carry ten times the impact of the Industrial Revolution.

Outside, students protested the meeting and questioned who should be shaping AI policy, with a further protest planned on the UNC campus for Wednesday morning. The town warned residents to expect heavy law enforcement presence around the Carolina Inn. That is the same constraint the speakers inside were describing, expressed politically rather than technically.

The finance track ended differently. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who chaired the Asheville meeting at the Omni Grove Park Inn alongside Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh, said nineteen members agreed and one did not. Treasury issued a chair's statement instead of a communique.

Asked to name the holdout, Bessent identified the People's Republic of China as the dissenter, describing it as the country with the world's largest and unsustainable current account surplus. According to CNBC, China objected to language on non-market economies exporting cheaply, to paragraphs praising IMF surveillance of global imbalances, and to text singling out countries where a meaningful share of external debt is owed to G20 members.

Set the diplomacy aside. IMF surveillance, external debt exposure, and the definition of a distorting trade practice are the items that determine sovereign risk, currency exposure, and landed cost for anyone operating across borders. They are still open, and they will be open in Miami.

Hold the two results next to each other. In one room China would not put its name to a paragraph about trade imbalances. In another, roughly two hundred and fifty miles away on the same day, it reportedly put its name to a framework shaping how twenty economies will regulate the most consequential technology of the decade. Governments can be immovable on the thing that costs them money this quarter and accommodating on the thing that constrains them in five years.

What it means for planning

The global rulebook for technology is fragmenting, and the fragmentation looks like the stable condition rather than a phase that resolves. The temptation is to read a light-touch declaration as relief. It is closer to the opposite.

Divergence is the planning assumption, not the risk case

A reported U.S. and China endorsement of the same framework does not narrow the problem. It moves it. If a large bloc of signatories runs a light-touch regime and the EU runs a codified one, a company deploying the same model in both is maintaining two compliance postures for one product. That does not get cheaper by waiting for the picture to settle, and there is no reason to expect it will. The practical move is to build governance to the strictest regime you actually operate under and treat the looser ones as a subset of it. One control framework mapped across multiple jurisdictions costs materially less than parallel frameworks maintained separately, and it is far easier to defend when a regulator or a customer asks how the system is controlled.

An endorsement is not a rulebook

Even a fully confirmed Chinese signature would commit Beijing to a posture, not to a text anyone has read. The principles are non-binding, unpublished, and unenforceable, and a government that signs and then stands up a new AI authority breaks nothing. Treat this as a signal about direction of travel, useful for anticipating where regulators are heading, and not as an input you can build a control framework against. Nothing here changes what you owe a customer or a court today.

The binding constraint is power and people, not policy

Two of the most aggressive AI builders in the world spent their time at a policy summit talking about megawatts and electricians. If your roadmap assumes capacity and skilled labor arrive on your timeline, that assumption deserves a hard test now. Interconnection queues, substation upgrades, and trade labor availability are the schedule risk. Model selection is not.

The absence of a regulator is not the absence of liability

Contract law, sectoral regulators, consumer protection, and negligence claims continue to operate whether or not a dedicated AI authority exists. Reading no new rules as no new exposure conflates two different things, and the gap between them tends to surface in a dispute rather than an audit.

Watch what the finance track could not agree on

Debt and imbalance language failing at the ministerial level is a signal about sovereign and trade volatility that reaches procurement, hardware sourcing, and capital planning well before it reaches the business pages.

Complexity is expensive; simplicity creates velocity

This week added complexity. A voluntary framework with no published text and a contested signatory list, a communique that did not happen, and a December summit in Miami that has to reconcile both. The organizations that come out of this well will be the ones that stop treating regulatory clarity as a prerequisite and start designing systems that hold up whichever way it goes.
